THE THREE FRONTIERS

Before these methods existed, nobody could prove whether ancient sacred texts shared a common source or just borrowed surface vocabulary. Each frontier answers a different question with hard numbers, honest limits, and reproducible code.

🕸️
Frontier I
Conceptual Network Mapping
What was already proven: Six sacred corpora—KJV Bible, Book of Enoch, Gospel of Thomas, Corpus Hermeticum, Dead Sea Scrolls, and Nag Hammadi—share vocabulary at rates far above random chance. The Gnostic texts alone showed 50.4% vocabulary overlap with each other, while literary controls (Shakespeare, Moby Dick) showed no such clustering. That proved a common source language existed, but it did not prove the texts shared deep meaning.
What this frontier tested: Do the texts reuse the same concepts, or just the same words? We built a directed semantic graph using an ontology of five esoteric themes—Angelology & Watchers, Gnosis & Divine Light, Archons & World Rulers, Radical Cosmic Dualism, and Ascent & Celestial Spheres. For each corpus we counted concept occurrences, computed pairwise cosine similarities, and compared the result against literary controls.
Key Finding: Hermetica ↔ Nag Hammadi keyword overlap = 0.971, but deep semantic similarity = 0.109. That gap is the entire point: these texts sound alike because they borrow the same theological vocabulary ("light," "spirit," "truth"), but they apply those words to fundamentally different cosmologies. The only pair where both methods agree is KJV ↔ Hermetica (0.669 keyword vs 0.674 semantic), which suggests a genuine thematic bridge between Jewish wisdom and Hermetic traditions.
Boundary of the evidence: The deep semantic scores come from a Sentence-Transformer trained on modern English web text, not ancient theological language. They measure "thematic alignment in modern semantic space," not absolute truth. Results are based on 20 text chunks per corpus (~5–10% of each corpus). Full-corpus embeddings may shift these values.
🔬
Frontier II
Forensic Stylometry
What was already proven: The Book of Enoch is not a single unified work. It contains internal contradictions, duplicate passages, and abrupt shifts in tone and vocabulary that scholars had long suspected indicated multiple authors. But without quantitative measures, that suspicion remained an opinion.
What this frontier tested: Does Enoch's writing behave like one uniform voice, or does its language change by section? We used four complementary methods: Type-Token Ratio (vocabulary richness per 500-word window), function-word profiling (100-function-word vectors with cosine similarity), burstiness analysis (clustering coefficient of thematic words), and hierarchical Ward-linkage clustering on 156 chapter-level segments.
Key Finding: Enoch's vocabulary is bursty—"spirits" (0.654), "portal" (0.646), "sheep" (0.615) cluster in tight sections rather than distributing evenly. The hierarchical dendrogram shows 31 clusters at threshold 0.3, 11 at 0.5, and 6 at 0.7. That pattern supports the documentary hypothesis: discrete source documents were stitched together by later redactors, and the clustering boundaries give statistical shape to those proposed divisions.
Boundary of the evidence: The 300-segment sliding-window analysis and the 156-segment chapter-level analysis are complementary, not identical. The Dead Sea Scrolls and Nag Hammadi corpora contain web-contamination artifacts (HTML/CSS terms like "button," "primary," "style" appear as top burstiness terms), which means those results should be re-run after corpus cleaning.
📜
Frontier III
Lacuna Reconstruction
What was already proven: The Dead Sea Scrolls contain 45 known lacunae (gaps where ink has faded or parchment is damaged) in the 1QS Manual of Discipline alone. Before this work, those gaps were simply blank—scholars could guess contextually but had no systematic method to generate testable predictions.
What this frontier tested: Can a Hebrew-native language model offer plausible completions where a manuscript is damaged? We extracted 200-word context windows around each lacuna and queried two models—a trigram language model and AlephBERT (`onlplab/alephbert-base`)—for top-k predictions. Text normalization converts ASCII Hebrew transcription to clean Unicode Hebrew before inference.
Key Finding: AlephBERT was successfully loaded for Hebrew-native reconstruction. The trigram model produces contextually appropriate predictions. 45 lacunae were identified and processed in 1QS Manual of Discipline.
Boundary of the evidence: Performance depends critically on text normalization. The 1QS transcription uses mixed ASCII/Latin format that must be standardized to pure Unicode Hebrew for optimal results. Current predictions are limited by the specialized transliteration system and small context windows. In the initial test batch, all 20 lacunae returned identical top-5 predictions, indicating the model is not yet effectively utilizing context on this specialized DSS transliteration—human review of every prediction is required.
