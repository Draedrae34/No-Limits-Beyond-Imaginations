💡 Key Findings

🚨 Critical Discovery: Keyword Overlap vs. Deep Semantics

The most significant finding of this research is that vocabulary overlap massively inflates perceived similarity between texts. When using Sentence-Transformers deep embeddings instead of keyword counting, many pairs that appeared highly similar (0.8-0.9) drop to near-zero or negative correlation:

Text Pair Keyword Similarity Deep Semantic Similarity Difference
Thomas ↔ Nag Hammadi 0.942 0.007 -0.935
Hermetica ↔ Nag Hammadi 0.971 0.109 -0.862
KJV ↔ Dead Sea Scrolls 0.892 0.066 -0.826
Enoch ↔ Nag Hammadi 0.809 -0.017 -0.826
KJV ↔ Enoch 0.941 0.668 -0.273
Interpretation: These texts share vocabulary but not meaning. They use similar theological language (light, spirit, truth) to describe fundamentally different cosmologies and soteriological systems. This validates the scholarly distinction between canonical, pseudepigraphal, and heterodox traditions.

📊 Stylometry: Enoch's Composite Nature

Burstiness Analysis reveals that Enoch's vocabulary is highly non-uniform. Words like "spirits" (0.654), "portal" (0.646), and "sheep" (0.615) cluster in specific sections rather than distributing evenly. This supports the documentary hypothesis that Enoch was stitched from multiple sources/authors over centuries.

🏛️ Reconstruction Feasibility

The trigram model trained on our 6-corpora corpus successfully predicts contextually appropriate words for 45 lacunae in the 1QS Manual of Discipline. However, BERT's performance is degraded by the mixed Hebrew/Latin script in the transcription. The path forward is AlephBERT/HebBERT fine-tuning on normalized Hebrew DSS transcriptions.

🕸️ Network: Idea Migration Patterns

The conceptual network reveals strong bridges between the Dead Sea Scrolls and both canonical (KJV: 0.892) and non-canonical (Enoch: 0.936, Nag Hammadi: 0.907) corpora. This positions DSS as a conceptual hinge in Second Temple Judaism, mediating between orthodox and heterodox traditions.
